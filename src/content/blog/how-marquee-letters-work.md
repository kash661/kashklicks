A marquee letter is a big hollow letter, usually built from aluminum, steel, plywood or MDF, with light bulbs set into its face. Wiring inside links every bulb to one cord, and the cord plugs into a wall outlet. It is the same trick theatre signs were using in the 1930s, made to stand in a party room instead of over a theatre entrance.

[Our rental letters](/services/marquee-letters/) are 4 ft tall with warm white bulbs, and they run from a regular wall outlet using extension cords we provide. I am a photographer first, so the guide ends with how to photograph lit letters without losing the faces around them.

**Six things to know**

- **Made of:** metal (aluminum or galvanized steel) or wood (plywood or MDF). DIY letters use foam board or coroplast.
- **Bulbs:** small globe bulbs in sockets through the face, either LED or old style filament.
- **Power:** a regular wall outlet. How many letters one circuit can run depends on the bulbs.
- **Setup:** a flat, level floor near an outlet, out of walkways, with a wall behind.
- **Outdoors:** depends on the build. Ours are made for indoor use.
- **Photos:** lights down, flash off, faces first.

## What are marquee letters made of?

Most marquee letters are made of metal or wood, with the bulbs set into the front face and the wiring tucked inside. Lighter letters for tabletops and DIY projects use foam board, coroplast, acrylic or even cardboard.

**Metal.** Aluminum or galvanized steel, sometimes stainless steel. A factory typically cuts the face, forms and joins the sides, drills the bulb holes, fits the sockets and wiring, then paints or powder coats the letter and runs a lighting test.

**Wood.** Plywood or MDF. One maker, for example, builds its 4 ft letters from half inch plywood fitted with a 12 bulb light string.

### The parts of a marquee letter

- **The face:** the front panel, drilled with a hole for each bulb.
- **The returns:** the side walls that give the letter its depth.
- **The sockets and wiring:** behind the face, running to one cord. In metal letters, an insulating collar keeps each socket and bulb base from touching the metal edge.
- **The back:** often left open on rental letters so the wiring can be reached, which is why they look best against a wall or backdrop.

Some letters skip the drilled face and mount the bulbs inside an open channel instead. Sign makers call that an open pan channel letter, and the Paramount Theatre in Seattle spells its name in them.

## Why they look like an old theatre sign

The name comes from the theatre. A marquee is the canopy over a theatre entrance, and the classic one is framed in white or yellow bulbs that flash or chase each other around the edge. Theatre historian Ben M. Hall called the movie marquees of the 1930s "electric tiaras." Even earlier, around 1900, a stretch of Broadway in New York was so bright with electric signs that it earned the nickname the Great White Way.

## How the bulbs light up

Each bulb screws into a socket fixed through the face, and wiring behind the face links the sockets to one cord. String lights with screw in bulbs are wired in parallel, and letters built on them work the same way. Each bulb gets its own full supply, so one burnt out bulb does not take the rest down with it.

### Globe bulbs, LED or old style

The classic marquee bulb is a small round globe, often 3 to 4 cm across, on the same small screw base as a night light bulb. Some letters use C7 or C9 bulbs, the shape of old Christmas lights. Either way, the bulb is LED or old style incandescent, and that choice matters:

- **Power.** An old style globe this size typically draws 5 to 7 watts. An LED version draws about 0.6 to 1 watt.
- **Heat.** Incandescent bulbs turn about 90 percent of their energy into heat, and the glass can get hot enough to burn skin. LEDs still make a little heat, mostly at the base, but run far cooler.

The look can come close. When Seattle's Paramount Theatre built a replica of its old marquee, it swapped 11 watt border bulbs for 0.75 watt LEDs chosen to closely match the old light, and the energy savings were estimated at about 90 percent.

### Warm white, cool white and colour

The colour of white light is measured in kelvin (K). Lower numbers look warmer and more golden, higher numbers cooler and bluer. ENERGY STAR groups roughly 2,200 K to 3,000 K as soft or warm white, but no fixed number sits behind those words, so brands differ a little. Colour changing letters usually run through a control box rather than straight from the wall. Ours use warm white bulbs.

## What power marquee letters need

The short answer is a regular wall outlet, which in Canada supplies 120 volts. Many rental letters plug into each other, so only one cord runs to the wall. Some run their bulbs at 12 or 24 volts through a small plug in adapter, which lowers the shock risk on the parts people touch, although the adapter still plugs into household power.

### How many letters can share one outlet?

That depends on the bulbs and on everything else on the same circuit. Most general purpose household circuits in Canada are 15 amps at 120 volts, which is 1,800 watts at most. For things left on for hours, the usual planning number is about 80 percent of that, around 1,440 watts, shared by everything on that circuit: the letters, and maybe the DJ, the uplights or a space heater. A breaker that keeps tripping is the warning sign.

Old style bulbs use far more of that budget. Some rental companies use 8 to 15 watt bulbs, while small LED globes draw about 1 watt or less. So limits differ: one rental chain says a standard outlet can run up to nine of its letters and needs more power beyond that, while another says one outlet runs its whole display. Where letters plug into each other, follow the maker's limit on how many can be joined, usually printed on a tag near the end plug.

## Safety around 4 ft light up letters

Most of this is common sense, but it helps to have it written down, especially with kids around.

- **Keep them upright.** No leaning, sitting or climbing. A 4 ft letter can tip if it gets bumped hard, so watch little ones and pets.
- **Keep things off the bulbs.** Drinks, jackets, balloons, flowers and fabric stay clear, and nothing gets draped over the letters or taped to them.
- **Leave them in place.** Do not move them or fiddle with the bulbs. If a cord looks damaged or feels hot, unplug it at the wall.
- **Run cords along walls.** Where a cord has to cross a path, lay it flat, tape it down and follow your venue's rules. Many venues ban duct tape but allow gaffer tape, which is cloth tape made to come off clean.
- **Unroll, never hide, never join.** Health Canada says to unroll cords completely and never run them under rugs or through doorways. Ontario's Electrical Safety Authority (ESA) warns that coiled extension cords can become very hot and may cause a fire, and says not to string extension cords together.

### What about outdoors?

Rental companies disagree, because it depends on how the letters are built. Some allow outdoor use only under cover, and some say to unplug the moment it rains. Wind is the other big risk for anything 4 ft tall: companies that set up outside may weigh letters down or move them in when it picks up, and one will not set up outdoors once gusts pass about 32 km/h. ESA also says never to use an indoor extension cord outside, even briefly, and to plug outdoor connections into a GFCI protected outlet.

## How to photograph marquee letters

This is the part I know best. Lit letters are tricky because the bulbs are far brighter than the faces near them, and a camera left to itself gets one of the two wrong. Here is what I would tell any host.

1. **Lights down, flash off.** The glow reads best when the room is darker than the bulbs, and flash washes it out. Keep bright windows beside you, not behind the letters, or the camera exposes for the window and faces go dark.
2. **Plan where people stand.** A 4 ft letter is shorter than most of the people posing with it, so whoever stands in front of the V makes it disappear. Put people beside the word or in the gaps between letters. For a portrait, do the opposite: move people a few steps in front of the letters and switch to Portrait mode, and the bulbs melt into soft round glows behind them.
3. **Faces first, then the bulbs.** Touch and hold on a face to lock focus and exposure there (AE/AF Lock on an iPhone), then slide the exposure down until each bulb is a round glowing dot, not a white blob. A little too dark beats a little too bright, because shadows lift later and blown out bulbs do not. If faces need more light, add soft light to them, like a lamp beside the group, rather than turning the room lights back up.
4. **Keep the word straight.** Hold the phone level at about chest height with every letter in frame. Tilting makes tall letters lean. If the word does not fit, step back rather than tilting. If it looks small, walk closer rather than pinching to zoom.
5. **Hold still in Night mode.** In a dim room your phone may switch to it on its own, and a capture can take a few seconds. Brace the phone or your elbows on something solid, ask everyone to freeze for a beat, and try the shot with Night mode on and off. Wipe the lens first, since a smudge adds haze around bright bulbs.
6. **Let warm be warm.** Warm bulbs look warm on camera, and that is part of the charm. If skin turns too orange, lower Warmth a little when you edit, and avoid mixing window daylight and bulb light on the same faces.
7. **Seeing stripes?** That is flicker from lights pulsing too fast for you to notice, and some LED and other plug in lights do it. On a camera, set the shutter to 1/60 or 1/120 of a second, which is Sony's advice for places on 60 Hz power like Canada.
8. **Photographers, drag the shutter.** Set the shutter around 1/30 to 1/60 and the room about 1.5 to 2 stops under, and let the flash light the faces while the bulbs keep their glow. A half CTO gel on the flash helps skin match the warm bulbs.
